Polish citizenship by descent when your ancestor left before 1920

Most American, Canadian and British families with Polish roots descend from emigrants who left before the First World War. That does not rule you out, but it makes two questions decisive: did the ancestor ever become a Polish citizen, and did they take another citizenship first?

Nobody was a Polish citizen before 1920

Poland regained independence in November 1918, and its first citizenship act took effect on 31 January 1920. Before that date your ancestor was an Austrian, Russian or German subject, however Polish their family was. Descent can only start from someone who held Polish citizenship after 1920.

Who became Polish in 1920 without living in Poland

The 1920 Act made someone a citizen if, on that date, they were registered in a commune inside the new Polish state and held no other citizenship. An emigrant living in New York or Chicago could still qualify through that registration:

  • Austrian partition (Galicia): a right of domicile (prawo przynależności) in a Galician commune.
  • Russian partition (Congress Kingdom): an entry, or the right to an entry, in the commune's population books (NSA II OSK 172/07).
  • Prussian partition: the Treaty of Versailles rules apply, and they generally required living in the territory. Emigrants from Poznań, Pomerania or Upper Silesia are hard cases.
  • Places that stayed German (Breslau/Wrocław, Stettin, East Prussia) or became the Free City of Danzig do not count.

What breaks the line

  • Naturalisation before 31 January 1920. An ancestor who already held US, Canadian or British citizenship then never became Polish. Check the date on the naturalisation certificate or petition, not the arrival date.
  • Naturalisation from 1920 to 18 January 1951. This ended Polish citizenship. Men still liable for Polish military service only had the loss postponed, at the latest to 1950 (see rule 3).
  • Children born abroad before 1920 with their birth country's citizenship never became Polish (NSA II OSK 1176/16). If your line runs through a US-born grandparent born in 1915, that line is closed. A sibling's line born after January 1920 may still work.
  • A father's loss before 1951 also ended the citizenship of his children under 18 (1920 Act, art. 13).

Records that decide these cases

  • A birth or baptism record from the Polish parish or civil registry.
  • Proof of registration: a Galician domicile extract or a population-book entry.
  • The naturalisation file (US: Declaration of Intention, Petition, Certificate), or proof that no naturalisation took place, such as the 1930 and 1940 census answers and alien registration.
  • Strong evidence of citizenship after 1920: a Polish passport, consular registration or Polish military registration.
